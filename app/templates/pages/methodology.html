Methodology

How CogniTrack Works

The research behind the five assessments and the Cognitive Composite Index (CCI)

About CogniTrack

CogniTrack is a self-guided, five-domain cognitive assessment — Memory Recall, Focus & Attention, Decision Making, Thinking Speed, and Visual Reasoning — built to give you an honest, informational snapshot of how your mind is performing today, and how that changes over time. This page explains how that snapshot is built, so you don't have to take it on faith.

Research Philosophy

Every scoring decision in CogniTrack traces back to one of two things: a published study, or an explicit engineering default chosen because no study gave us a better basis to decide. We never blend the two silently. Where a task is grounded in published research, we say which paper and what it found. Where a choice is ours alone — like weighting the five domains equally when scoring your overall profile — we say that too, instead of dressing up a judgment call as science.

How the Assessments Work

Each of the five modules is inspired by a well-established cognitive testing paradigm:

  • Memory Recall — a recognition-memory and sequence-span task, in the tradition of the Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test and the Corsi Block-Tapping Task.
  • Focus & Attention — a simple reaction-time task, in the tradition of the Deary-Liewald reaction time programme.
  • Decision Making — a colour-word interference task, in the tradition of the Stroop task.
  • Thinking Speed — a timed symbol-matching task measuring speed and accuracy together.
  • Visual Reasoning — a mental rotation task, in the tradition of Shepard & Metzler's classic 3-D rotation paradigm.

"In the tradition of" is a deliberate phrase: these are original, browser-based implementations inspired by the cited paradigms, not licensed or clinically validated replications of them. The Scientific Inspiration section below is explicit about what each source paper does and doesn't support.

CCI Overview — What the Score Actually Is

The Cognitive Composite Index (CCI) is computed fresh every time you view your dashboard — it's never a hidden formula, and nothing about it is stored as a permanent label on your profile. It's built in four steps:

  1. Raw data — the numbers your assessment already produced (reaction times, recognition hits, sequence length, and so on).
  2. Normalized score (0–100) — each raw number is scaled to a 0–100 range using a plausible performance range, so a memory score and a reaction-time score can sit on the same axis.
  3. Domain score — where a domain has more than one relevant metric, they're averaged equally. This is a neutral default, not a claim that (say) recognition accuracy matters more or less than sequence span — no source paper ranks them against each other.
  4. Overall score — the five domain scores, averaged equally for the same reason.

What CCI does not do: it does not compare you to a population norm or percentile (we don't have a trustworthy normative dataset to compare against), it does not adjust your score based on age, gender, or education (the source papers give correlations, not the regression formulas that would be needed to do that honestly), and it never claims clinical or diagnostic validity for any of the five games.

Confidence — Why It Exists

Alongside every score, CogniTrack shows a Confidence level — High, Moderate, or Low. Confidence answers a different question than the score does: not "how did you perform," but "how much should today's number be trusted as representative."

Things like stress, sleep quality, caffeine, distractions, medication, and a shorter trial count than the original research protocols used can all make a single session's result noisier — without making you any less capable. That's why these factors are only ever allowed to move your confidence label, never your score itself. A stressful day can lower confidence in today's Attention score; it can never lower the score. If you'd like the specific reasons behind a domain's confidence level, expand "Why this score?" on that domain's card on your dashboard.

Scientific Inspiration

Seven papers were read in full and directly shaped the design of these assessments. Expand a domain below to see the source, what it established, and how directly it applies to CogniTrack's implementation.

Memory Recall

Kessels et al. (2000), The Corsi Block-Tapping Task: Standardization and Normative Data — the direct basis for the sequence-span portion of Memory Recall.

Farrahi et al. (2023), Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test normative data — supports the recognition-memory scoring logic; note the original RAVLT list is longer (15+15 words) than CogniTrack's 11-word list.

Focus & Attention

Deary, Liewald & Nissan (2011), the Deary-Liewald reaction time task — the direct basis for measuring mean reaction time; their validated protocol uses 20 scored trials, CogniTrack's uses 5, which is why short trial counts lower confidence rather than the score.

Decision Making

MacLeod (1991), Half a Century of Research on the Stroop Effect — the basis for scoring the interference effect (the slowdown caused by a conflicting cue) as the core measure, rather than raw accuracy, which ceilings too quickly on a simple task to carry much information.

Thinking Speed

Sandry & Ricker (2022), Motor Speed Does Not Impact the Drift Rate — supports scoring a combined speed-and-accuracy "efficiency" measure instead of accuracy alone, since accuracy-only scoring can miss real differences in processing speed.

Visual Reasoning

Shepard & Metzler (1971), Mental Rotation of Three-Dimensional Objects — the classic basis for the mental-rotation paradigm. This module scores an accuracy + response-time composite rather than the paper's true headline measure (how response time scales with rotation angle) — see Limitations below for why.

Limitations

  • Scores are scaled against an engineering-judgment plausible range, not a population-normed percentile — no source paper supplied a normative dataset trustworthy enough to build one.
  • Several modules use fewer trials than their original research protocols (e.g. 5 attention trials vs. the validated 20), which is reflected as lower confidence, not a lower score.
  • Age, gender, and education are not used to adjust any score — the available research gives correlation coefficients, not the regression equations that would be needed to do so responsibly.
  • Visual Reasoning currently scores an accuracy + speed composite rather than the angle-vs-time rotation rate Shepard & Metzler's paradigm is best known for — with only three angle bands and a handful of trials per band today, that slope would be too statistically fragile to report.
  • None of the five modules are validated clinical or diagnostic instruments. See our Terms of Use.

Future Validation

Two improvements are on the roadmap once enough data exists to support them responsibly: a within-user trend comparing a new session against your own prior average (needs no external norms at all, and is arguably the most defensible number this system could show), and revisiting the Visual Reasoning rotation-rate slope once trial counts are large enough for it to be statistically stable. Neither is available yet — we'd rather ship nothing than ship a number we can't stand behind.
